**ON SCREEN TEXT:**

**CAPTION:**
1️⃣. The study is called "Treating Thoughts as Material Objects Can Increase or
Decrease Their Impact on Evaluation."
Universidad Autónoma de Madrid and Ohio State. Psychological Science, 2013.

2️⃣. Participants wrote down negative thoughts about themselves.
One group physically tore up the paper and threw it away.
One group kept the paper and protected it.
One group only imagined discarding it.

3️⃣. The group that physically threw the paper away showed a drop in how much that
thought influenced their self-evaluation.

influence went up.
The imagining group barely moved.
The hands did the work. Not the insight. Not the analysis.

4️⃣. This is the mechanism underneath every grief ritual humans have ever built.
Burial. Scattering ashes. Burning letters. Stones into a river.

## V03 — Freud Was Wrong And Knew It

**ANGLE:** Authority collapse + a genuinely devastating narrative beat (his private

**CITATION:** Freud, S. (1917). "Mourning and Melancholia." Freud's 1929 letter to

**ON SCREEN TEXT:**
For 80 years therapists taught that healthy grief means severing the bond with the
dead. Freud wrote that. He also privately admitted it was wrong — and never corrected

**CAPTION:**
1️⃣. In 1917 Freud published "Mourning and Melancholia."
His argument: grief ends when the mourner withdraws all emotional energy from the
person who died. Cut the cord. Move on. He called it decathexis.
That one paper set the standard for Western grief treatment for eight decades.

2️⃣. Then his daughter Sophie died.
Years later he wrote privately to a friend: "We find a place for what we lose... it
remains something else. And actually this is how it should be. It is the only way of
perpetuating that love which we do not want to relinquish."
He never went back and corrected the published theory.

3️⃣. It took until 1996. Klass, Silverman and Nickman published Continuing Bonds.
They found the healthiest bereaved people did not sever the bond. They transformed
it — kept a relationship with the dead through objects, ritual, conversation, place.
The people who forced themselves to "let go" did worse.

4️⃣. So the goal was never to stop carrying them.
The goal is to stop carrying them as a weight and start carrying them as a witness.
That distinction is the whole difference between grief that calcifies and grief that
integrates. Ritual is how you make the switch, because ritual is the only tool that
lets you *act out* a change in relationship instead of just deciding on one.

5️⃣. The protocol:

conversation.
• Speak each one out loud, then release it into moving water.
• Keep the final stone. That's the continuing bond. You didn't let them go. You

**CAPTION:**
1️⃣. Mary-Frances O'Connor spent two decades running neuroimaging on the bereaved.
Her book is The Grieving Brain.
Her finding: grief is not primarily an emotion problem. It's a learning problem.

2️⃣. Your brain encodes the people you love into its predictive model of the world —

3️⃣. Which is why being *told* they're gone changes nothing.

4️⃣. That's exactly what ritual is for.
A ritual is an experience you construct on purpose to feed the predictive system new
data: the object was here, I held it, I released it, it's gone, I'm still here.

**ON SCREEN TEXT:**
When she published it in 1987 the field called it pseudoscience for over a decade. The

**CAPTION:**
1️⃣. 1987. Francine Shapiro notices that moving her eyes side to side while holding a
distressing thought reduces its charge. She builds it into a protocol: EMDR.

2️⃣. The field went after it hard.
Researchers called it pseudoscience — repackaged exposure therapy with a theatrical
gimmick bolted on. Papers were published specifically to discredit the eye-movement

Today EMDR is recommended for PTSD by the World Health Organization, the US Department
of Veterans Affairs and the American Psychological Association.

3️⃣. The argument about *why* it works is still live, and that part is honest. Some
researchers think bilateral stimulation loads working memory while the memory is
active, weakening its hold. Others think it's the structured exposure doing the work.
But nobody argues about this part anymore: a repeated physical action, performed while
deliberately holding a painful memory, changes that memory's grip.

4️⃣. Which is the exact structure of every grief ritual ever built.
Hold the memory. Perform a repeated physical action. Complete the sequence.
Walking to water carrying a stone, speaking a memory, releasing it, walking back — is

The oldest version of the technology and the newest one have the same shape.

5️⃣. The protocol:
