Prolonged Grief Disorder: Symptoms and How to Heal It

Note: This content is not a substitute for professional therapy or medical care. If you are in crisis, please reach out to a mental health professional.

Your grief feels like it will never end. This isn't just sadness; it's a deep, relentless ache. If your loss happened over a year ago and you're still stuck, you might be experiencing prolonged grief disorder. This post will show you what it is and how to start moving through it.

Key Takeaways

  • Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) is a distinct diagnosis from typical grief or major depression.
  • PGD involves persistent intense longing, preoccupation, and feeling lost without the deceased, lasting over a year for adults.
  • Healing PGD requires active engagement with emotional completion rituals to process and integrate the loss.

What exactly is Prolonged Grief Disorder?

Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) describes grief that extends beyond what is typical in terms of intensity and duration. It is not just sadness. It is a persistent, debilitating state that profoundly impacts your daily life.

Normal grief eventually softens and integrates. PGD, however, feels like the acute phase of grief has frozen in time. The world feels unreal without the person you lost.

The American Psychiatric Association added PGD to the DSM-5-TR in 2022. This recognition highlights its distinct clinical significance. It is a real and identifiable condition.

Roughly 7-10% of bereaved adults experience PGD. This statistic shows it is more common than many people realize. Recognizing it is the first step toward healing. (Source: American Psychiatric Association, 2022)

What are the main symptoms of Prolonged Grief Disorder?

The core symptom of PGD is a persistent intense longing or preoccupation with the deceased. This feeling dominates your thoughts and emotions, making it hard to focus on anything else.

You might experience an intense emotional pain related to the death. This can include sadness, guilt, anger, or difficulty accepting the loss. The pain feels constant, not ebbing and flowing.

Other key symptoms include a sense of identity disturbance. You might feel like a part of you died with the person. You might also struggle to feel pleasure or find meaning in life since the loss occurred.

A feeling of detachment from others is common. You might feel isolated, even when surrounded by loved ones. You could also experience intense loneliness or emptiness.

PGD is diagnosed when these symptoms last for at least 12 months for adults after the death. For children and adolescents, the threshold is 6 months. These feelings must cause significant distress or impairment in your daily functioning. (Source: PubMed, 2021)

This is not just 'taking a long time to grieve.' It's a specific set of symptoms that interfere with living. Your brain is struggling to process the permanent absence. (Source: NIH, 2022)

How is Prolonged Grief Disorder different from depression?

While PGD and major depression share some symptoms, they are distinct. Depression is characterized by a pervasive low mood, anhedonia, and a lack of interest in life generally.

PGD's distress, however, centers specifically on the deceased and the circumstances of the death. The pain directly relates to the loss, rather than a generalized despair about life itself.

Someone with PGD might still find joy in other aspects of life, just not in ways that involve the deceased. A person with depression experiences a broader inability to feel joy.

Brain imaging studies show distinct neural patterns in individuals with PGD compared to those with depression. This indicates different underlying mechanisms. (Source: Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2023)

It is possible to experience both PGD and depression simultaneously. However, treatment approaches can differ significantly based on the primary diagnosis. Understanding the distinction helps target healing efforts.

What specific steps can help heal prolonged grief?

Healing prolonged grief requires active, intentional engagement. It is not about forgetting the person. It is about integrating the loss and completing the emotional process.

The Emotional Completion Ritual provides a framework for this. It helps your system acknowledge the permanence of the loss while releasing the trapped emotional energy.

One powerful approach is somatic release. Grief, especially when prolonged, gets stuck in the body. Rituals help to move this stored energy.

Here is a simple practice, The Grief Stone Release, adapted from my Stone Release Ritual, to begin this work:

**The Grief Stone Release:**

1. **Find Your Stone:** Go outside and find a small, smooth stone. Hold it in your hand. Feel its weight and texture.

2. **Imbue Your Grief:** Close your eyes. Imagine all the heavy, stuck grief, the intense longing, and the feelings of unreality, flowing from your body into the stone. Let it absorb the pain.

3. **Acknowledge the Pain:** As you hold the stone, consciously acknowledge the depth of your sorrow and the reality of your loss. Speak it aloud: 'I feel this loss deeply.'

4. **Release the Burden:** Go to a natural body of water, like a river, lake, or ocean. Stand at the edge. With intention, gently toss the stone into the water.

5. **Observe the Release:** Watch the ripples. Imagine the water carrying away the emotional burden you just released. This is not forgetting, but releasing the *stuckness*.

This type of ritual helps create a clear psychological boundary. It signals to your nervous system that a shift is possible. These are not magic fixes, but powerful steps.

Rituals provide a structure for processing intense emotions. They help move us from a state of internal overwhelm to a sense of external action and release. (Source: PMC, 2020)

How do I find support and move forward with my life?

Finding the right support is crucial for healing PGD. This might include a grief-informed therapist, support groups, or trusted friends and family who understand.

It is important to find people who can hold space for your grief without trying to 'fix' it. You need acknowledgment, not dismissal.

Rebuilding your life does not mean replacing the person you lost. It means finding new ways to incorporate their memory while creating new experiences and connections for yourself.

Consider engaging in activities that bring you a sense of purpose or meaning. Even small steps, like volunteering or exploring a new hobby, can help you reconnect with life.

Healing from prolonged grief is a process. Be patient and kind to yourself. Each step you take, no matter how small, moves you closer to completion and peace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PGD a new diagnosis?

Yes, Prolonged Grief Disorder was officially added to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) in 2022. This formalized criteria for a condition that clinicians have observed for many years. Its inclusion reflects a growing understanding of complex grief reactions and the need for specific diagnostic pathways to better support those affected.

Can children get PGD?

Yes, children and adolescents can experience Prolonged Grief Disorder, although the diagnostic timeframe is shorter. For individuals under 18, the symptoms of PGD must be present for at least six months following a loss, rather than 12 months for adults. It is essential for parents and caregivers to be aware of these signs and seek appropriate support for children who are struggling with grief.

How long does normal grief last?

Normal grief varies greatly from person to person, and there is no strict timeline. It typically involves intense pain in the initial months, which gradually lessens over time as the individual adapts to the loss. While strong feelings can resurface around anniversaries or holidays, healthy grief usually shows a trend toward integration and finding new meaning within about a year. Prolonged grief is when this integration does not happen.

What if I feel guilty about moving on?

Feeling guilty about moving on is a common experience in grief, especially with prolonged grief. It can feel like a betrayal of the person you lost. However, moving forward with your life is not about forgetting or replacing them; it is about honoring their memory by living fully. Completing your emotional process allows you to carry their love and lessons without being burdened by relentless pain. This is a path to deeper connection, not abandonment.

Is therapy necessary for PGD?

Therapy can be highly beneficial for individuals experiencing Prolonged Grief Disorder, especially grief-focused therapy approaches. A qualified therapist can provide tools and a safe space to process the complex emotions and cognitive patterns associated with PGD. While not every individual requires therapy, it offers structured support that can be vital for moving through stuck grief. Consider it a powerful option for deep healing.

How does emotional completion help PGD?

Emotional completion helps PGD by providing a structured way to acknowledge and release the unresolved emotional energy tied to the loss. PGD often involves trapped grief that cannot fully process the permanence of death. Rituals like The Emotional Completion Ritual help the body and mind create psychological closure. This allows the individual to integrate the loss into their life story without it dominating their present. It is about finding a way to carry the love without the lingering pain.

What if my grief started years ago?

It is absolutely possible to heal prolonged grief, even if it started many years ago. Grief does not have an expiration date, and neither does the potential for healing. The core work remains the same: identifying the stuck emotional patterns and engaging in rituals that help to complete the unresolved aspects of the loss. It might take sustained effort, but freedom from the lingering pain is attainable at any point in your healing journey. Your path to peace is always open.
