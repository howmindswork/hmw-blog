Does Open Breathwork Release Stored Grief and Trauma?

Note: This content is not a substitute for professional therapy or medical care. If you are in crisis, please reach out to a mental health professional.

Your body stores grief and trauma, often without your conscious awareness. Open breathwork offers a direct path to release these deeply held emotions. It's a powerful tool for somatic healing, helping you move through what's stuck. This practice can be a vital part of your emotional completion process.

What is Open Breathwork and How Does it Work?

Open breathwork is a conscious, continuous breathing practice. It uses specific breath patterns to activate the parasympathetic nervous system. This helps release stored emotional tension and bring suppressed feelings to the surface. It can induce non-ordinary states of consciousness.

This practice bypasses the usual mental gatekeepers. It allows you to feel emotions directly in your body. The breath acts as a bridge between your conscious mind and your deeper emotional landscape. This can be a profound experience for many.

Your breath directly impacts your nervous system. Conscious, connected breathing can shift you from a state of fight or flight to one of rest and digest. This shift creates a safe internal environment for processing difficult emotions. It's a key component in the Emotional Completion Ritual.

How Does Breathwork Connect to Emotional Release?

Open breathwork creates a unique physiological state. It alters blood gas levels, which can temporarily quiet the prefrontal cortex. This allows access to deeper emotional layers often held in the body. It helps bypass mental blocks to feeling.

When you breathe continuously, you activate your vagus nerve. This nerve plays a critical role in regulating your stress response. Increased vagal tone is associated with better emotional regulation and resilience. (Source: Frontiers in Psychology, 2020).

Many people carry unresolved emotions as physical tension. This tension can manifest as chronic pain, stiffness, or unexplained discomfort. Open breathwork creates movement and release in these areas. It helps the body complete stress cycles that were previously interrupted.

The breath allows for a natural discharge of energy. This discharge might manifest as tears, tingling, or physical shaking. These are natural somatic responses to emotional release. It's your body's way of letting go of what it's been holding.

Can Open Breathwork Address Grief and Trauma?

Yes, open breathwork is highly effective for processing grief and trauma. It helps bypass the rational mind's defenses. This allows the body to complete incomplete stress responses. It can bring clarity to unspoken or unfelt aspects of loss.

Trauma often leaves an imprint not just on the mind, but deep in the nervous system. Conscious breathing can help renegotiate these imprints. It provides a way to re-experience and release frozen responses safely. This can be crucial for healing.

Many individuals report significant shifts in their relationship with past events. Breathwork creates a safe container for difficult memories and emotions to surface. It allows you to witness and release them without being overwhelmed. (Source: Journal of Experiential Psychotherapy, 2017).

Grief isn't just a mental process; it's a full-body experience. Unprocessed grief can manifest as physical symptoms, fatigue, or a sense of emotional numbness. Breathwork can help thaw these frozen places, allowing grief to move through you.

What is a Simple Open Breathwork Practice?

A simple open breathwork practice involves conscious, connected breathing. You breathe without pause between inhale and exhale. Focus on a full breath into the belly and chest. This helps create a continuous flow of energy and sensation in the body.

This practice aims to create a gentle, continuous cycle. It's not about hyperventilating, but about creating an unbroken stream of breath. The goal is to bring awareness to your respiratory system. It supports the release of emotional blockages.

Here is a simple practice called The Rhythmic Release Breath:

1. Lie down comfortably. Place one hand on your belly, one on your chest. Feel the rise and fall.

2. Inhale deeply through your mouth, filling your belly first, then your chest. Make it a full, expansive breath.

3. Exhale fully through your mouth, letting go completely. Let out any sound naturally.

4. Connect the inhale and exhale without pause. Create a continuous, rhythmic circle of breath. Avoid holding your breath at any point.

5. Breathe rhythmically for 10-15 minutes. Allow any sensations, emotions, or sounds to arise without judgment. Simply observe what comes up.

6. When ready, return to your natural breath. Rest quietly and observe the after-effects. Integrate the experience.

This type of breathwork is a powerful step in completing emotional cycles. It can unlock feelings you didn't know you had. This practice is a cornerstone of deep emotional healing. It aligns with principles from healing emotional numbness.

What Should You Consider Before Trying Open Breathwork?

Open breathwork can be powerful. It's important to be in a safe, supportive environment. Consider any medical conditions like pregnancy, heart issues, or severe mental health conditions. Always listen to your body and go at your own pace.

Certain health conditions may be contraindications for intense breathwork. These include uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma, severe asthma, or a history of psychosis. Always consult with your doctor before starting new practices. This ensures your safety.

Working with a trained facilitator is highly recommended, especially when processing trauma. A skilled guide can hold space for difficult emotions. They can help you integrate the experience effectively. This support is invaluable for deep healing.

Integration is key to lasting change. After a breathwork session, allow yourself quiet time to process. Drink plenty of water and practice gentle self-care. This helps your nervous system settle and embed the insights gained. It completes the cycle of release and renewal.

Remember, breathwork is a tool for self-exploration. It's not a quick fix, but a practice that supports ongoing emotional health. Approach it with respect and curiosity. Your body holds wisdom. (Source: University of California, Berkeley, 2021 review on breath practices).

Frequently Asked Questions

Is open breathwork the same as regular deep breathing?

No, open breathwork is different from regular deep breathing. It involves a continuous, connected breath without pauses between inhale and exhale. This specific pattern creates physiological changes that can lead to deeper emotional release, unlike typical relaxation breathing exercises. It's an active practice designed for processing, not just calming.

How long does a typical open breathwork session last?

A typical active open breathwork session often lasts between 20 to 60 minutes. This is usually followed by a period of integration and rest, which can last another 15-30 minutes. The entire process, including preparation and debrief, might take 90 minutes or more in a facilitated setting. It varies depending on the practitioner and the individual's needs.

What might I feel during an open breathwork session?

During an open breathwork session, you might experience a wide range of sensations and emotions. These can include tingling, numbness, warmth, or coolness in your body. You might also feel intense emotions like sadness, joy, anger, or fear. Physical movements, trembling, or vocal releases are also common. It's important to allow these experiences without judgment.

Are there any risks associated with open breathwork?

Yes, there are potential risks, especially for individuals with certain health conditions. People with a history of cardiovascular problems, high blood pressure, glaucoma, severe mental illness, or pregnancy should exercise caution. Always consult a healthcare professional before starting. It is best to practice with a trained facilitator, particularly if you are new to it or processing trauma.

How often should I practice open breathwork?

The frequency of open breathwork practice depends on your individual needs and the intensity of your sessions. Some people practice weekly or bi-weekly for deeper processing. Others use it monthly or as needed for emotional regulation or release. It is not generally recommended for daily practice due to its intensity; regular, gentle breath practices are better for daily use.

Can open breathwork help with anxiety and depression?

Many individuals report that open breathwork significantly helps with anxiety and depression. By releasing stored emotional tension and regulating the nervous system, it can reduce feelings of overwhelm. It helps access and process underlying emotional causes. However, it should be seen as a complementary practice and not a replacement for professional mental health treatment.

What should I do after an open breathwork session?

After an open breathwork session, it is crucial to allow time for integration. Rest quietly, drink plenty of water, and avoid strenuous activities immediately afterwards. Reflect on any insights or emotions that arose without trying to force meaning. Journaling or talking with a trusted friend or facilitator can also be helpful. This period allows your nervous system to rebalance and absorb the experience.
